Methods

Initial and final stress

σv0 = Σ γ·h (total, to mid-sublayer) u0 = γw·(z − zw) below groundwater σ′v0 = σv0 − u0 σ′f = σ′v0 + q (Δσ = q at all depths)

The load is treated as very wide, so the stress increase does not reduce with depth. Water pressure is hydrostatic below the groundwater level, with γw = 9.81 kN/m³. Ref: Holtz & Kovacs (2011).

Immediate settlement

Si = q·H / M M = E(1−ν) / [(1+ν)(1−2ν)]

This is one-dimensional elastic compression. It applies mainly to free-draining and unsaturated layers. Use ν = 0.5 on saturated clay: with no lateral strain and no volume change, its immediate settlement is zero. Immediate settlement follows the load through the loading period, and the surcharge part recovers fully at removal. Ref: Poulos & Davis (1974); Holtz & Kovacs (2011).

Primary consolidation

σ′f = σ′v0 + q σ′p = OCR·σ′v0 or σ′v0 + POP σ′f ≤ σ′p Sp = H/(1+e0)·Cr·log(σ′f/σ′v0) σ′v0 ≥ σ′p Sp = H/(1+e0)·Cc·log(σ′f/σ′v0) otherwise Sp = H/(1+e0)·[Cr·log(σ′p/σ′v0) + Cc·log(σ′f/σ′p)]

Each clay layer is split into equal sublayers and the settlements are summed. Set sublayers to 1 to match a mid-layer hand calculation. Logs are base 10. Ref: Terzaghi (1943); Holtz & Kovacs (2011).

Rate of primary consolidation

Tv = cv·t / Hdr² Hdr = H/2 (two-way) or H (one-way) U(Tv) = 1 − Σ 2/M²·exp(−M²·Tv) M = (π/2)(2m+1), m = 0, 1, 2 …

This is Terzaghi’s solution for uniform initial excess pore pressure. The end of primary, tp, is the time at the U you select: Tv = 0.848 at 90%, 1.129 at 95% and 1.781 at 99%. Ref: Terzaghi (1943).

Secondary compression

Ss = Cα/(1+ep) · Hp · log(t/tp) for t > tp ep = e0 − (1+e0)·Sp/H Hp = H − Sp

Secondary compression starts at the end of primary in each layer (Hypothesis A). Cα is the change in void ratio per log cycle of time. The small primary remainder after tp is still counted. Ref: Mesri & Godlewski (1977); Ladd et al. (1977).

Loading period

t ≥ tc S(t) = S0(t − tc/2) t < tc S(t) = (t/tc)·S0(t/2)

This is Terzaghi’s correction for a linear load ramp, where S0 is the curve for instant loading. Set the loading period to 0 for instant loading. Ref: Terzaghi (1943).

Temporary surcharge

t ≤ tr S(t) = Sp(q+qs)·U(t) + Ss U needed = Sp(q) / Sp(q+qs) After removal, in each sublayer, σ′r is the stress reached at U(tr): σ′r ≥ σ′f rebound = H/(1+e0)·Cr·log(σ′r/σ′f) Ss = Cα′/(1+e0)·H·log(t/tr) σ′r < σ′f primary continues under q Ss continues with Cα

Rebound develops with U(t − tr) using the same cv. Remaining primary continues from the equivalent time on the permanent-load curve. Cα′ defaults to Cα·Cr/Cc, and the reduction is a planning assumption, so check it against site data or set your own ratio. Ref: Johnson (1970); Mesri et al. (1997).

Embankment creep

S = (α/100) · He · log(t′/t1) for t′ > t1 t′ = t − tc

This is log-time creep of the fill under its own weight, added to the foundation settlement. He is the thickness at the location and tc its loading period. Only compression of the fill after t1 is counted, because earlier movement is treated as part of construction. A surcharge doesn’t change the creep. Surface load q is a separate input, so check that it represents the embankment. Ref: Sowers et al. (1965).

Total settlement and conventions

S(t) = Si(t) + Sp(t) + Ss(t) + Se(t) immediate + primary + secondary + embankment creep
  • Times count from the start of loading. Settlement after handover is S(td) − S(th).
  • Free-draining layers give immediate settlement only. Clay / silt layers give all three foundation parts.
  • γ is one bulk unit weight per layer. Split a layer at the water table if its weight differs above and below.
  • Results are preliminary estimates and need review by a qualified geotechnical engineer before use in design.

Limitations

What the methods leave out, how that could affect your results, and what you can do about it if it matters for your project.

Very wide, uniform load (no stress spread)

Likely overestimates beneath edges and narrow loads

Impact. Δσ = q at every depth, which is only true under the middle of a load much wider than the compressible layers are deep. Settlement under batters, narrow embankments, footings and nearby structures will be smaller than shown, and differential settlement across the width is not given.

To address. Work out stress with depth using Boussinesq, Westergaard or 2:1 spread, and enter a reduced, averaged q at locations near edges as an approximation. Or use 2D or 3D numerical analysis where edges, structures or differential settlement matter.

No lateral movement or stability check

Could underestimate on soft ground

Impact. True 1D loading has no shear distortion, so immediate settlement of saturated clay is zero. Real embankments on soft clay also settle from undrained shear and lateral spreading, and can fail if they are built too fast.

To address. Check embankment stability separately, using limit equilibrium for the end of construction and for each stage. Allow for undrained distortion settlement, often estimated from elastic or numerical analysis, and use staged construction with inclinometers where the factor of safety is low.

No vertical drains (radial consolidation)

Overestimates time to consolidate if drains are used

Impact. Only vertical drainage through the layer is modelled. With wick drains or sand drains, consolidation is much faster than shown, so surcharge removal times and settlement after handover will be wrong.

To address. Use a radial consolidation method such as Barron (1948) or Hansbo (1981), including smear and well resistance, and combine it with vertical drainage using Carrillo’s rule. Confirm with piezometers and settlement plates.

Small strain and constant parameters

Either way, more error in very soft soils

Impact. Cc, Cr, cv and E are held constant in each unit, and the e–log σ′ lines are straight. In very soft clay, organic soil or peat with strains above about 10–20%, cv falls as the soil compresses, and settlement and timing can be noticeably off.

To address. Split thick layers into several units with depth-varying values. Run sensitivity cases with high and low parameters (copy a unit to try alternatives). For large strains, use large-strain theory such as Gibson et al. (1967), or software that varies parameters with stress.

Layers consolidate independently

Either way for stacked clay layers

Impact. Each clay layer drains on its own using the drainage you set. Where clay layers touch without a free-draining layer between them, the real drainage path is longer and consolidation is slower than shown.

To address. Combine touching clay layers into one layer with one-way or two-way drainage as appropriate, or use multi-layer consolidation software. Check for continuous sand lenses, which shorten drainage paths.

Secondary compression assumptions

Either way, large spread in long-term values

Impact. Secondary compression starts at tp (Hypothesis A) with constant Cα at all later times and stresses. Long-term settlement is sensitive to Cα, tp and design life, and the Cr/Cc reduction options are empirical.

To address. Measure Cα at the design stress range using long-duration oedometer increments. Check against Cα/Cc ratios, report a range rather than one value, and back-analyse settlement monitoring as it becomes available.

Single-stage linear loading

Either way for staged or complex programmes

Impact. Load rises linearly over the loading period, then stays constant. Staged construction, pauses, later extra loads or partial removal are not modelled, apart from the single surcharge.

To address. For staged loading, superpose stages by hand or in a spreadsheet, or use consolidation software. Model a later load change as a separate location to bracket its effect.

Groundwater and submergence

Usually overestimates where fill settles below water

Impact. Water pressure is hydrostatic from one fixed level. As fill settles below the water table it becomes buoyant, so the effective load falls. Drawdown, which adds effective stress, and artesian or under-drained pore pressures are not modelled.

To address. Reduce q by γw times the expected settlement below the water table, and iterate. Run separate cases for high and low groundwater. Use software with a pore pressure profile where groundwater is not hydrostatic.

Surcharge removal uses average U per layer

Could underestimate residual settlement

Impact. All sublayers share the layer’s average degree of consolidation. Near the mid-plane of a thick clay layer, consolidation lags the average, so some primary settlement can remain after removal even when the check says it is covered.

To address. Allow a margin on removal time, or on the surcharge height. Decide removal from field data: piezometers showing excess pore pressure has dissipated, and settlement plates fitted with the Asaoka (1978) method.

Embankment creep is empirical

Either way, depends heavily on α and t1

Impact. Fill creep uses one α and t1 for the whole site, independent of fill height, stress or moisture. Collapse settlement of fill on wetting, poor compaction and degradation of weak rock fill are not included.

To address. Specify and test compaction. Derive α from monitoring of similar fills, and test sensitivity to t1. Assess collapse potential for dry, loosely placed or cohesive fill.

Elastic immediate settlement

Either way for granular soils

Impact. E is constant in each layer, but granular soil stiffness rises with stress and falls with strain. Correlations from CPT or SPT to E are uncertain, often by a factor of 2 or more.

To address. Split thick granular layers to let E rise with depth. Compare correlations from more than one test type, and run high and low E cases.

Ground variability between locations

Either way between investigation points

Impact. Each location uses its own profile, with one value per unit. Ground conditions between locations are not interpolated, and the colours on the plan show only results at the points.

To address. Add locations where the ground or loading changes. Use characteristic or cautious design values, and fill gaps in the investigation where differential settlement matters.

Organic soils and peat

Could underestimate long-term settlement

Impact. Peat and highly organic soils have very high Cα, large strains, and can decompose over time. This method describes them poorly.

To address. Get specialist testing and advice. Use large-strain and creep-based methods, and consider removing or treating the peat.

Software checking

Applies to every result

Impact. This calculator is not a validated or certified design tool. Input mistakes, unit errors or software faults could go unnoticed.

To address. Do an independent hand check of at least one location (sublayers = 1 matches a mid-layer hand calculation). Have the inputs and results peer reviewed, and record the version number shown on the Methods & information tab.

Results are preliminary estimates. They need review by a qualified geotechnical engineer, and checking against site monitoring, before use in design.
